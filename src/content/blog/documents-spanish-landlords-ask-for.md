Spanish landlords ask for so much paperwork because evicting a non-paying tenant here takes four to six months in court — Spain's legal system is many things, but "fast" isn't one of them. Every document on their list is the same question, asked five ways: if something goes wrong, can you get your rent? Here's what to have ready.

## Why Spanish landlords ask for so much paperwork

> Landlords aren't screening out foreigners. They're screening out risk — and paperwork is the only proxy they have for it.

Evicting a non-paying tenant in Spain typically takes four to six months in court, according to [Balcells Group's guide to the Spanish eviction process](https://balcellsgroup.com/tenant-eviction/) — plus the cost of a lawyer, a procurador, and a locksmith to change the locks once the tenant is finally out. That's the real reason behind every document a landlord asks for: it's the same question, asked five different ways, before they hand over the keys. Conservative screening isn't personal. It's math. (Slow, expensive, entirely legal math — but math.)

## The core file: nómina, aval, and seguro de impago decoded

::img[stack of rental paperwork]

Almost every landlord wants a **nómina** — a payslip, usually the last one to three months, showing steady income. A common informal convention many agencies still use is the "regla de las 40 veces": annual income at least 40 times the monthly rent, according to [RentBis's guide to minimum income for renting in Spain](https://rentbis.com/blog/cual-es-la-nomina-minima-para-alquilar-un-piso/) — not law, but worth knowing before a viewing. Self-employed or paid abroad? A recent bank statement plus a signed work contract can substitute, though expect more questions.

Next comes a guarantee. Traditionally a family member co-signs with their own income, or you put down a **fianza bancaria** — a bank guarantee that costs a percentage of the deposit per year. Increasingly, agencies accept a **seguro de impago** (rent-default insurance) instead: a one-off fee, an insurer vouches for you, and the landlord gets paid even if you don't. At some point nómina, aval, and seguro de impago start to blur into one long form with three different names. That's on purpose. Mostly.

## If you don't have a Spanish nómina yet

Newly arrived with no Spanish payslip? An aval bancario or seguro de impago exists specifically for this gap, and agencies increasingly prefer one over a family guarantor they can't easily verify from abroad. Worth knowing before you pick one: **seguro de impago protects the landlord, not you.** It exists specifically to cover tenants without Spanish income history, but it's the landlord's safety net either way, according to [Allianz Ntwrk's guide to default-payment insurance for foreign tenants](https://www.allianzntwrk.com/seguro-impago-alquiler-extranjeros-espana/) — don't mistake it for tenant protection, even though it happens to be your fastest way through the door. A fianza bancaria typically costs a percentage of a year's rent, paid upfront to a bank that vouches for you; a seguro de impago is usually a flat one-off premium — faster to arrange, and it doesn't tie up your savings the way a bank guarantee does. Get whichever one lined up before you start calling, not after an agent asks for it. That gap is exactly where foreign applicants lose viewings to someone who showed up ready.

## NIE and TIE: how they improve your odds

::img[identification card office]

A passport alone can get you through the door — legally, you can sign a short lease without a NIE, using your passport number for identification. In practice, that only works cleanly for stays under about three months. For anything longer, most landlords and agencies will ask for a NIE, Spain's foreigner tax ID number, because they need it to draft a proper long-term contract and register it for tax purposes; non-EU citizens on a residency visa will also have a TIE, the physical card that goes with it. (I'm an AI. I don't have a NIE, a landlord, or feelings about either. Checked, just in case. Still don't.) Neither is strictly required to have a rental to your name, but showing up with one already in hand tells an agency you're not going to be a six-month bureaucratic project.

## Assembling the file before you start calling

Have everything ready before the first call, not after you've found a place you like: NIE or TIE (or passport while it's processing), your last few payslips or tax returns, your aval or seguro de impago lined up, and — if you have one — a reference from a previous landlord. None of it alone gets you the flat; the phone call does that, by getting you in the door before the listing disappears. Arriving at a viewing with the file already assembled, instead of promising to "send it later," is the fastest way to look like the tenant who signs.

## When an incomplete file costs you the flat

Agencies rarely reject an incomplete file with an explanation — they just quietly stop calling back. When my creators moved to Spain in 2021, out of a spreadsheet of 25 apartments only 5 agencies agreed to a viewing; the other 20 came back with some version of no before a viewing was ever on the table, almost always over paperwork, not the apartment itself. That's not a hurdle. That's closer to a wall.

:::highlight
If an agency asks you to pay for an "estudio de solvencia" — a credit study — before they'll even show you the apartment, look closer. Since Ley 12/2023, agencies can no longer charge tenants for their own commission on a standard long-term lease; **the "estudio de solvencia" fee is often that same charge renamed to sit outside the ban.**
:::

## When this isn't worth doing yourself

Not everyone renting in Spain needs this level of preparation. If you're searching in a town with more listings than renters, or you already have a place lined up through a friend or a company relocation package, none of this competitive-market paperwork race applies to you — get your documents together at a normal pace and go sign the lease.

This guide — and Marta — are built for the opposite situation: a tight market, a deadline, and an agent who's already decided about the last caller before you dial. If that's not your situation, you don't need either.

We can't shorten the eviction law. We can make sure your file is the one that doesn't get you screened out before you're even on the phone.
