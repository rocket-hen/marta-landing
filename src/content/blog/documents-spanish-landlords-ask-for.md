Spanish landlords are conservative, not because they dislike foreigners, but because eviction law here is slow and expensive when a tenant stops paying. Every document they ask for is really the same question asked five different ways: if something goes wrong, can I get my rent?

## The core file

Almost every landlord wants a **nómina** — a payslip, usually the last one or two, showing a salary that's at least three times the monthly rent. If you're freelance or paid abroad, a recent bank statement and a signed work contract can substitute, but expect more questions.

Next is an **aval** — a guarantee. Traditionally a family member co-signs with their own income, or you put down a **fianza bancaria**, a bank guarantee that costs a percentage of the deposit per year. Increasingly, agencies accept a **seguro de impago** (rent-default insurance) instead: you pay a one-off fee, an insurer vouches for you, and the landlord gets paid even if you don't.

Add a copy of your **DNI/NIE or passport**, and if you're not an EU citizen, proof you're allowed to live in Spain for the length of the contract.

## What actually moves the needle

None of these documents alone gets you the flat — the phone call does that, by getting you in the door before the listing disappears. But arriving to a viewing with your file already assembled, instead of promising to "send it later," is the fastest way to look like the tenant who signs.

> Agencies remember who showed up prepared. They don't remember who promised to email something.
