Renting in Spain costs more upfront than the monthly rent alone, and almost nothing explains what that total actually looks like. On a realistic €1,000-a-month apartment, the honest range is €2,000 to €4,000 just to get the keys — the deposit, the first month, and one fee that a lot of tenants pay by mistake because it doesn't legally apply to them. Here's the full math, plus the separate bar you need to clear before any of it matters.

## The first month's rent

This is the one nobody needs a guide for, and it's still worth stating plainly, because a total that skips a line item isn't a total. The first month's rent is paid at signing, alongside the deposit — not after you've moved your boxes in, not on a grace period. Two payments land on the same day, and the second one is where most people's mental math falls apart.

> Knowing the real number doesn't make it smaller. It just means nothing on moving day is a surprise.

## The deposit: one month mandatory, up to three total

::img[cash envelope keys]

The legal deposit — fianza — for a primary residence is one month's rent, and it's mandatory, not negotiable downward. On top of that, a landlord can ask for up to two additional months as extra guarantee (garantía adicional), which means three months total is the legal ceiling. Not four, not "whatever the landlord decides feels safer" — three, full stop, contract or no contract.

When my creators moved to Spain in 2021, paying a full year of rent upfront wasn't treated as a red flag or a negotiating tactic — it was just how it worked if you actually wanted the apartment. A three-month deposit sounds steep until you remember what it used to take to compete for a listing at all.

The deposit comes back within one month of handing over the keys, minus real damage only — normal wear doesn't count, whatever the outgoing inventory list tries to claim.

## The fee you probably don't owe

Since Ley 12/2023, agency and contract-management fees for a primary residence are the landlord's cost, not yours. This is the single line item most likely to trip up an expat's budget, because it's exactly what's normal to pay in a lot of other countries — so when an agency asks for it, it doesn't sound wrong, it sounds routine. It isn't. If it's charged for a standard residential lease, it's illegal, whatever it's called on the invoice — some agencies rename it "solvency study" or "legal advisory" specifically so it doesn't sound like the fee the law banned. [We cover the full version of this, including what to say when an agency pushes back, here](/blog/negotiate-rent-and-fees-in-spain/).

The one exception: temporada (seasonal) rentals aren't covered by this protection, so if that's the kind of contract on the table, the fee conversation is genuinely different.

## If your profile needs a guarantee

::img[bank paperwork signing]

Not everyone pays for this. A salaried tenant with stable income and enough tenure is often approved on payslips alone — no guarantee needed, no extra cost. If your profile doesn't clear the standard checks, budget for one: an aval bancario has a real upfront cost to you (an opening fee, and with some banks, your own money frozen for the length of the lease — still yours, just not spendable). A seguro de impago costs you nothing directly, since the landlord pays the annual premium, but you still have to pass the insurer's solvency check before it exists at all. [The full comparison, including which one actually favors you, is here](/blog/spain-rental-guarantees-explained/).

## The full picture: a worked example

On a €1,000-a-month apartment, here's what walking in the door can actually cost:

| Line item | Cost |
|---|---:|
| First month's rent | €1,000 |
| Deposit (mandatory minimum) | €1,000 |
| Deposit (if landlord requests the full legal maximum) | up to €2,000 more |
| Agency fee, primary residence | €0 |
| Guarantee, if your profile needs one | varies — see above |

:::highlight
**The legal ceiling on a security deposit for a primary residence is three months, total** — one month fianza plus up to two months garantía adicional, and nothing above that is legal on a standard housing contract (LAU art. 36). Knowing the ceiling won't make a landlord ask for less. It does mean you'll recognize the moment someone's asking for more than the law allows.
:::

Add it up and the realistic range is €2,000 at the low end — one month's rent, one month's deposit, no fee, no guarantee cost — to €4,000 if the landlord requests the maximum deposit and your profile needs a paid guarantee on top. That's not a typo-proof single number, because there isn't one. It's a range with the variables labeled, which is more useful than a figure that turns out to be wrong the moment your situation doesn't match whoever quoted it.

## The income rule, separate from the cash

Having the cash on day one doesn't automatically clear you — there's a second bar, and it's about income, not savings. The near-universal unofficial rule is net annual income around 40 times the monthly rent, which works out to a little over three times the rent per month. Nobody writes this into the contract, but almost every agent checks for it before booking a viewing, so it's worth knowing before you start calling rather than after the third agency asks the same question in a slightly different order.

Both bars matter. Plenty of applicants clear the cash total and still get passed over because the income math doesn't work, and the reverse happens too — a strong salary attached to someone who hasn't actually worked out what signing day costs.

Having the number is the easy part.
