If a landlord in Spain asks for an "aval bancario" or a "seguro de impago" and you don't have a Spanish payslip to fall back on, you've just hit one of the more confusing parts of renting here. Both are guarantees against the landlord's risk, but only one of them actually asks anything of you directly. This guide explains what each one is, who pays for what, and which is more likely to work in your favor.

## What these two terms actually mean

An aval bancario is a bank guarantee — your own bank issues it to the landlord, backed by your money or your standing as a customer. A seguro de impago is a rent-default insurance policy, but the landlord is the one who takes it out, with an insurance company, not you.

> Both guarantees protect the landlord. Only one of them asks anything of you directly.

That's the part that trips up most newcomers: you'd assume a "tenant guarantee" means you're the one arranging it, the way a security deposit works. With the seguro de impago, you're not the customer — you're the person being screened so someone else's insurance policy can go through.

## Who pays for each one, and why it's confusing

::img[bank document signing]

The aval bancario, you arrange and effectively pay for yourself — usually an opening fee, and with some banks, funds held or frozen for the length of the lease. The seguro de impago is contracted and paid for by the landlord, who covers the annual premium directly.

That split creates a genuine asymmetry. You never hand over money for the insurance policy, but you're still the one who has to clear its screening before it exists at all. The landlord puts up the money; you carry the risk of being turned down. (The insurer does not send you a thank-you card for that, for the record.)

## What it actually costs you

The aval bancario has a direct cost to you: an opening fee, and at some banks, your money sitting frozen for as long as the lease runs. The seguro de impago looks free from where you're standing — but like any insurance premium, someone's paying for it, and that cost can end up part of the rent conversation.

A seguro de impago typically runs 3-5% of the annual rent. On a €1,000-a-month rent, that's €360-600 a year the landlord is taking on to reduce their own risk — and that margin is sometimes exactly the edge you're missing in a negotiation.

## How the tenant screening actually works

::img[calculator rental documents]

Insurers apply different requirements depending on your employment situation. Salaried employees typically need a permanent or long-term temporary contract, at least 6-12 months of tenure, and their last 2-3 payslips. If you're self-employed (autónomo), insurers usually want you current with the tax authority and social security, at least a year of activity, and your income tax return alongside recent quarterly filings. Pensioners and civil servants tend to count as low-risk with just proof of income.

One rule applies across the board: your rent can't exceed 35-40% of your net income, and you can't appear on Spain's debtor registries — ASNEF and RAI, two databases lenders and insurers check for unpaid debts, similar in spirit to a credit blacklist. The aval bancario works differently: it depends more on your relationship with your own bank than on any standardized rule, since each bank sets its own criteria.

## Which one fits your situation

If you have savings but an unconventional work profile — recently self-employed, variable income — an aval bancario can get approved where a seguro de impago would reject you for insufficient tenure. If you'd rather not freeze money and your profile already clears the 35-40% rule, the seguro de impago is the more liquid option.

:::highlight
Since the landlord is the one paying the 3-5% premium, a borderline applicant can sometimes offer to effectively cover that cost themselves — for instance, accepting a slightly higher rent that offsets it. **The landlord doesn't lose the insurance money, and you turn an automatic risk-profile rejection into an actual negotiation.** It's not a guarantee it works, but it changes the conversation from a flat no to a number.
:::

Neither guarantee replaces having your paperwork ready before you call. The right one only matters if you get to that conversation in time.

## When you might not need either

If you already have a permanent contract and enough tenure, you likely need neither — plenty of landlords accept payslips alone. In areas with more supply than demand, these extra guarantees often get requested out of habit rather than real necessity.
