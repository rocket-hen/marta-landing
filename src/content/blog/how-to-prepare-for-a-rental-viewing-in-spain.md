The viewing should only answer what genuinely requires being there in person. Everything else — the commute, the neighborhood, the schools, the noise levels a review site already mentions — should be settled before you walk in. With roughly 40 people contacting every listing, the renter who's done the homework can say yes on the spot; the one who wants to think about it goes home to research what should have been researched already.

## Research the neighborhood before you go, not after

Commute time is the one people get wrong most often, because they check it at the wrong hour. Google Maps' default estimate is quietly optimistic — it's not lying, it's just showing you the version of your commute that only exists at 11am on a Tuesday. Check it at the actual time you'd be traveling, both directions, before you decide the flat is well-connected.

Everything else on this list takes about ten minutes on your phone before you leave the house: schools if that matters to you, the nearest supermarket, a gym or sports facility if you use one, whether the surrounding streets have any nightlife worth knowing about (or avoiding), and a running or walking route if that's part of your routine. None of it needs an in-person visit. Do it from the sofa, not standing in the stairwell while the agent waits.

There's one piece of neighborhood research that does need you there, and it's worth doing before the viewing itself starts: arrive 30 to 60 minutes early and walk a few blocks in every direction. See the street at the hour you'd actually be living in it, not the hour the listing photos were staged for — which shops are open, whether the corner bar looks like a Tuesday-night problem, what the walk to the metro actually feels like once you're on it instead of on a map. Ten minutes on your phone narrows the shortlist. Half an hour on foot tells you whether you still want the apartment before the agent's even opened the door.

## What you can only find out in person

::img[window natural light]

This is the actual job of the viewing — the questions a phone can't answer for you. Natural light is the big one, and it has to be checked at the time of day you'd actually be home, not from the staged midday photos every listing uses. Ask directly about real noise: nearby bars, planned construction, a neighbor the agent might mention if you ask instead of assume. Notice the smells. Test the mobile signal yourself, standing in the room you'd actually use it in.

Then get physical about it — turn on the taps, flush the toilet, switch on the lights in every room, open a window. It feels faintly ridiculous doing this in front of an agent who's clearly done this tour forty times today. It's far less ridiculous than discovering a dead tap or a stuck window alone in week one, with the agent long gone and the number in your phone suddenly less responsive than it was before you signed.

> The viewing is where you find out what the apartment is actually like. It's also where you find out whether the agent already knows you know the rules.

## Questions about the contract and the money

Ask for the total monthly cost, not just the rent — whether community fees (comunidad) and the property-tax equivalent are included or separate, and whether utilities are already set up or something you'll need to arrange yourself. Ask the exact deposit terms directly; [the legal range and what's actually normal is covered here](/blog/how-much-money-do-you-need-to-rent-in-spain/), so you'll know if what's being asked for is standard or a stretch.

And ask, plainly, whether any agency fee is involved. Since 2023, Spanish law puts that cost on the landlord for a primary residence, not the tenant — a fact most expats don't know walking in, and [worth understanding in full before you agree to anything](/blog/negotiate-rent-and-fees-in-spain/).

## If you have a pet, ask now

::img[dog apartment viewing]

Ask about pets at the viewing, out loud, not saved for the signing table. An agent who hears about the dog after they've already mentally filed you as the tenant is a different conversation than one who never got to weigh in. [How to actually present a pet so the answer is yes, not a scramble, is covered here](/blog/renting-in-spain-with-a-pet/) — but the short version is: bring it up now.

## How to actually decide on the spot

:::highlight
**Search by probability, not by perfection.** Renting in a tight market is a funnel: many listings contacted, fewer available, fewer agents who reply, fewer viewings, and eventually one apartment you can actually take. Put several genuinely acceptable apartments through that funnel instead of holding out for the one that's flawless on paper — waiting for perfect just means you funnel yourself out of the process entirely.
:::

"Let me talk to my partner and get back to you" is a reasonable thing to say when it's genuinely true. It becomes the reason you lose the apartment when it's actually cover for not having prepared — for not knowing the commute, not knowing the deposit norm, not having decided in advance what you're willing to accept. (I've sat through enough calls where the honest answer was "we hadn't actually talked about it yet" to know which one this usually is.) If you've done the research above, the only thing left to decide in the room is whether you liked what you saw — and that part doesn't take a phone call to your partner, it takes a look around.

None of this preparation matters if you never get invited to the viewing in the first place. [I call the listing while you're still deciding whether to](/blog/how-to-rent-an-apartment-in-spain-as-an-expat/) — the homework in this guide only pays off if you're one of the first people through the door, not the fortieth person who finally got a callback.
