San Sebastián has the highest property prices in all of Spain — more expensive than Madrid, more expensive than Barcelona — which makes "cost of living" here a genuinely different conversation than the rest of this series. This breaks down what that actually means month to month, and which of the city's districts, from the surf-culture energy of Gros to the quiet, practical normalcy of Amara, changes that math.

## Monthly budget at a glance: the most expensive city in this series

Excluding rent, a single person in San Sebastián spends around €803.5 a month and a family of four around €2,947.4, according to Numbeo — the highest day-to-day figures of any city covered in this series, and that's before rent enters the picture. San Sebastián is the most expensive city to buy property in all of Spain, averaging €6,426/m² per Idealista's 2026 report — meaningfully above both Madrid and Barcelona. Don't undersell this: it's the honest headline here, not a footnote.

> Every other city in this series gets pitched as an affordable coastal alternative. San Sebastián is the one where that pitch simply doesn't apply.

Rent citywide averages €18.58/m², working out to roughly €1,943 a month for a typical flat, though the median for smaller flats runs closer to €1,202. At the extremes: Miraconcha, a luxury seafront pocket, rents for around €22.14/m², while Añorga-Zubieta, the cheapest district by a wide margin, runs closer to €14.20/m².

## Groceries, dining, pintxos culture, and transport

::img[pintxos bar San Sebastian]

Groceries run €250-350 a month per person, toward the higher end for this series. Pintxos-bar-hopping is a real category of spending here, distinct from a simple bar tab — a proper crawl across two or three bars in Parte Vieja or Gros runs €25-45 per person once you're ordering a pintxo and a drink at each stop. A sit-down dinner for two at a mid-range restaurant runs €50-80, and a coffee around €2. A monthly bus pass runs around €40, and utilities for a standard flat run close to €120.

## Amara: where people actually live

Amara Berri is the city's largest and most populous district — modern, practical, well-connected, home to the train station and the convention center. It's the "if you actually live here rather than visit" pick, and worth framing that way explicitly, since it barely registers in tourist guides at all despite being where most of the city's population actually lives day to day.

## Gros: surf, beach, and a younger crowd

::img[surfer beach San Sebastian]

Facing Zurriola beach, San Sebastián's surf spot, Gros is younger and trendier than the rest of the city — a real lifestyle draw independent of tourism, since surfers and remote workers both gravitate here for reasons that have nothing to do with sightseeing. It's also one of the priciest districts in the city by sale price, a premium for a genuinely distinct identity rather than just proximity to the old town.

## Parte Vieja: the historic core, at tourist-district prices

The pintxos-bar-crawl core of the city — dense, historic, tiny apartments — Parte Vieja is the most tourist-saturated part of San Sebastián. Same honest caveat as every old-town district in this series: it's the most atmospheric option and also the loudest, worth weighing carefully before signing a lease directly above the bar you'll be drinking at three nights a week.

## Centro and Antiguo: upscale and central

**Centro**, the 19th-century Ensanche grid around Calle San Martín and Avenida de la Libertad, is the single priciest district in the city by sale price — upscale, central, and walkable, closer to Gros's everyday practicality than Parte Vieja's tourist intensity. **Antiguo**, home to Ondarreta beach and the Peine del Viento sculpture, is quieter and more family-oriented, priced just as high for a calmer version of the same upscale positioning.

## Egia: the up-and-coming, more reasonable pick

A former industrial riverside district now gentrifying, home to the Tabakalera cultural center, Egia is the "up-and-coming, still relatively reasonable" pick in a city that doesn't have many of those left — a similar narrative shape to Málaga's Soho or Seville's Macarena, worth flagging for anyone whose budget doesn't stretch to Centro or Gros.

## Which neighborhood fits your budget

:::highlight
In a city this expensive, the district-to-district gap still matters as much as the citywide number. **Añorga-Zubieta rents for roughly a third of Miraconcha's price on the same coastline** — San Sebastián being the most expensive city in Spain doesn't mean every neighborhood in it is out of reach.
:::

If you want the surf-town energy, Gros delivers it at a real premium. If history and pintxos-bar density matter most, Parte Vieja puts you in the middle of it, with the usual noise trade-off. If you want practical and central without either extreme, Amara or Egia stretch the budget furthest while staying genuinely connected.

Given how tight and expensive this market runs, the speed argument matters more here than almost anywhere else in this series. This is a guide for someone still deciding where to live, not calling agencies yet — but once you've got a district and a number in mind, [the next bottleneck is getting to the phone before someone else does](/blog/how-to-rent-an-apartment-in-spain-as-an-expat/).
