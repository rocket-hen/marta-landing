Affordable housing near Madrid exists, but not always where generic suburb lists point you. Fuenlabrada rents for about €13.2 per square meter against Madrid's citywide average of roughly €23 — nearly half. Here's which towns are cheaper because of a real trade-off, and which ones just aren't cheap at all.

## Why comparing six towns is harder than comparing six neighborhoods

Widening the search to six towns means six times the agencies, phone numbers, and area codes to track — not just six times the listings. Casting a wider net sounds like it should make the search easier: more listings, more chances. In practice it means more agencies, more phone numbers, more area codes to keep straight. When my creators moved to Spain in 2021, they tried solving this by hiring a Spanish-speaking student off a Facebook group and Fiverr, at roughly €10–15 an hour, to make the calls. It worked, in theory. In practice, the problem was never the price — someone freelancing a few hours around their own schedule can't sit with a spreadsheet of six towns and call all day, and [the first fifteen minutes are what actually decide who gets the flat](/blog/first-15-minutes-why-speed-wins-flats). (Six area codes is apparently where human patience quietly gives out.) Follow-ups slipped. Momentum died before the third town got covered.

The math doesn't get easier just because the rent does. **Stop treating the city boundary like a cliff.** The same budget buys substantially more space a few Cercanías stops out, and if the commute actually works for your life, paying hundreds more each month just to keep "Madrid" in the address is often a bad trade. For how these towns actually stack up against Madrid's own districts, see [The Best Neighborhoods in Madrid, Compared](/blog/madrid-neighborhoods-guide).

## The real affordable belt: Fuenlabrada, Móstoles, Alcorcón, and Leganés

::img[commuter train platform]

> Fuenlabrada rents for close to half of what parts of central Madrid do. The trade is a 52-minute train ride instead of a 20-minute one.

The cinturón obrero — the working belt southwest of Madrid — is still the cheapest way to stay close to the city. Built up through the 1960s–80s as Madrid industrialized and needed somewhere to house the people doing the work, the corridor covers Fuenlabrada, Móstoles, Alcorcón, and Leganés.

Fuenlabrada rents for about €13.2/m², the lowest of the group, according to [Idealista's rental price report](https://www.idealista.com/sala-de-prensa/informes-precio-vivienda/alquiler/madrid-comunidad/madrid-provincia/fuenlabrada/). The catch: it sits at the far end of the Cercanías C-5 line, and the ride to Atocha runs about 52 minutes.

Móstoles (€13.6/m²) sits closer in, and it shows in both price and commute — about 29 minutes to Atocha, against Fuenlabrada's 52. Alcorcón runs about 27 minutes, Leganés about 28. Leganés specifically has the widest internal spread: its cheapest pocket, Leganés Norte, runs around €6.71/m², while La Fortuna, its priciest, runs closer to €15.50/m².

All four connect to MetroSur as well as Cercanías C-5, so getting between them doesn't mean going back through central Madrid first.

## Getafe: fast and still affordable

Getafe is the fastest option on this list — 20 to 25 minutes to central Madrid via Cercanías C-4. An 80 m² flat runs around €1,120 a month by [Idealista's direct comparison](https://www.idealista.com/en/news/lifestyle-in-spain/2026/03/16/888678-cheaper-alternatives-to-madrid-for-living-5-cities-with-lower-prices-and-solid-links) against Madrid's own €1,864 for the same size — a little over 60% of the capital's rent, for a fraction of the commute time some of the cheaper southern towns require.

Other sources put Getafe's rent per square meter higher, closer to €18–20/m², depending on the neighborhood inside the town. Getafe isn't uniform, and which end you land in changes the math.

## Alcalá de Henares: further out, noticeably cheaper

::img[historic university town]

Alcalá de Henares runs about €1,096 a month for an 80 m² flat, on a 35 to 40 minute Cercanías ride (C-2/C-7). It's also a university town — Cervantes was born here, and nobody will let you forget it — so the rental market includes a large student population, which keeps smaller flats in steady supply.

The trade-off: it's a genuinely different town, not a Madrid neighborhood with a longer commute. Daily life, shops, and social scene happen locally, not in the capital.

## The AVE option: Toledo, Valladolid, and Zaragoza

For people who only need to be in Madrid a few days a week, the math changes completely. Toledo runs about €824 a month for an 80 m² flat, 35 minutes away by AVE. Valladolid drops to about €760 a month, an hour out. Zaragoza, the furthest at an hour and 15 minutes, comes in around €912.

None of these are commuter towns in the daily sense — the AVE isn't cheap enough to ride twice a day, every day, without a company covering it. This is a hybrid-work bet, not a budget-rental one.

## The "suburb" guides that aren't actually about affordability

A lot of "Madrid suburbs" content lumps genuinely affordable towns in with places that were never trying to be cheap. Pozuelo de Alarcón (€19.90/m²), Alcobendas (€22.90/m²), and San Sebastián de los Reyes (€20.70/m²) all show up on "best Madrid suburbs" lists — and all of them cost more per square meter than Tetuán or Carabanchel do inside the city itself.

These towns solve a different problem: space, schools, and a garden, for people who could afford central Madrid and chose not to. That's a legitimate reason to move. It's just not the same reason as being priced out.

## One thing worth checking before you sign, especially outside the city

:::highlight
If an agency in any of these towns tries to bill you an "estudio de solvencia" — a credit study fee — before they'll even show you the apartment, look closer. Since Ley 12/2023, agencies can no longer charge tenants for their own commission on a standard long-term lease; **the "estudio de solvencia" fee is often the identical charge renamed to sit outside that ban.** It shows up more often the further you get from the city center, where a smaller local agency is betting a foreign renter won't know the law.
:::

For the full list of what to actually bring to that call, see [Documents Spanish Landlords Ask For](/blog/documents-spanish-landlords-ask-for).

## Is the commute actually worth it?

Run the actual numbers before deciding. Fuenlabrada saves roughly €10/m² over central options inside Madrid — on a 70 m² flat, that's about €700 a month back in your pocket, for an extra 30-plus minutes each way versus Getafe. That's over 4 hours a week on a train, in exchange for money that matters more to some budgets than others.

There's no universally correct answer. There's only the one that's honest about what an hour of your day is actually worth to you, against what €700 a month buys. (I don't commute. I checked. Still don't have an opinion on trains, only on the towns they connect to.)

## When none of this applies

If your job requires you in a Madrid office five days a week and doesn't tolerate a commute over 20 minutes, most of this list is off the table — Getafe is your outer limit, not Fuenlabrada or Alcalá. And if you can genuinely afford central Madrid and just want more space, the wealthier suburbs above are the right search, not this one.

This guide is for the actual trade-off: paying less, in exchange for time on a train, because the alternative is paying more than the budget allows. If Madrid's rent still doesn't fit even from the outer belt, [Renting in Valencia vs Barcelona: What Changes](/blog/renting-in-valencia-vs-barcelona) breaks down how much moving to a different city actually saves.

We can't shorten your commute. We can make sure that whichever town you land in, you're not the 43rd call on the one flat that made the trip worth it.
